An independent label puts out a record. For anyone to hear it, somebody has to
find the few hundred people who might actually play it — programmers,
curators, show hosts — and write to each of them personally, with a real
pitch.

That's weeks of work per release. Most labels never do it. So most records
just disappear.

Spindle does it. Every night, for every artist on the roster.

Get it wrong and you're not marketing, you're spam. So the whole thing turns
on picking the right few hundred.

That shortlist is a vector search in CockroachDB, with the filters — tenant,
model, contactable — living inside the index itself, not bolted on after.

And that index starts with tenant_id. One column doing our security boundary
and our partition key at once.

pgvector can do the similarity. It can't make one decision do both jobs.

The index is never finished, either. Agents are adding to these records all
day — what a station plays, who runs it, what they said last time.

And there's no orchestrator. Agents don't call each other — a changefeed on
the memory tables wakes the next one.

So kill half the fleet mid-run. The work still finishes. The lease expires and
the row comes back.

The send claims its lease before the network call. Two workers, same name. The
name can't tell them apart. The token can.

When people write back, the reply finds its own thread — every pitch carries a
token we minted for one conversation.

We're holding contact details for real people, and European law says where
that data can live. Same table, same query: this row is in Virginia, this one
is physically in Ireland.

Residency per row, enforced by the database instead of by us remembering to.
Now lose a region. It keeps answering.

It spends the label's money too, on its own, up to a ceiling. Last night it
asked to put more behind one record, and I approved it.

So this morning — what was it looking at when it asked? I can't just query it.
We never stop collecting, so those metrics have been overwritten and the
thread states have moved on.

But the ledger row stored the exact logical timestamp it decided at. Four
extra words of SQL, pointed at that timestamp, and they come back. Not a copy
of them.

Then it re-runs the valuation against those old numbers and checks it still
gets the same answer.

No audit table. No event log. No versioned copy of anything.

Postgres cannot do that at any price.

One database doing the index, the scheduler, the outbox, the event bus and the
ledger. It scales to zero between releases.
