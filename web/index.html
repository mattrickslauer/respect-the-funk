The market you're releasing into

Nine in ten tracks are commercially invisible.

253 million tracks sat on streaming services at the close of 2025, and 88% of them earned 1,000 streams or fewer. Another 106,000 arrive every day.

This is the shape of the market — not a marketing problem you can out-spend, but a distribution of outcomes. It's why the only sane strategy is more attempts at a lower cost per attempt.

The arithmetic

Paid streams lose money by construction.

This isn't a campaign that was run badly. It's what the unit economics do when you point ad spend at a royalty rate — the gap is structural, and no amount of creative fixes it.

Seeded momentum is repeatedly described by the people who sell it as contingent on continued spend. One campaign manager, on the record: visible paid saturation can actively suppress genuine adoption — "if every influencer is posting the song, some people don't want to use it." Treat seeding as buying paid content with an unproven multiplier. If you wouldn't buy the videos alone, don't buy the hoped-for cascade.

What the research actually supports

Three findings we built the product around.

Academic

Templatability is the mechanism — not audio quality.

Sounds spread when they imply a video a creator can copy. Hit-song science has failed repeatedly at the other explanation: 32,000 titles and 632 acoustic features could not learn popularity, and audio+lyrics explained only 1–8% of variance in a 646-entry study. The song is the substrate. The template is the product.

Network position predicts spread. The audio does not.

In hand-labeled TikTok virality analysis, creator follower count was the single strongest predictor and music was not significant at all. The lever worth pulling is how many distinct follower graphs a release enters — not how catchy the chorus is.

Constraint

Breakout is lottery-shaped. So the edge is cost per attempt.

The best-designed cultural-markets experiment ever run — 14,341 participants, 48 songs, 8 parallel worlds — found quality only weakly predicts success, and that adding social influence increases both inequality and unpredictability. The top 10% of songs take 96% of all TikTok creations. When outcomes are lottery-shaped, the edge isn't a better ticket — it's more tickets at a radically lower cost per ticket.

How it works

Create. Test. Measure. Repeat on what survived.

One loop, run per release against your actual roster — with the expensive step amortised across every song an artist ever puts out.

01 — Create

Templates, not finished ads.

Register an artist once — look, wardrobe, likeness consent. After that every song generates a pack of vertical, remix-ready templates in minutes, built to be imitated rather than admired. Multiple variants per release, deliberately, because you don't know which shape lands.

02 — Test

Through accounts we openly own.

Variants go out through a small network of disclosed, label-owned niche accounts — never covert, never impersonating fans. It produces the one thing seeding never does: a clean read on which template shape earns pickup.

03 — Measure

Only what's genuinely countable.

Sound-usage data is measured behaviour, not inferred demographics. Clicks are real. We report those, per variant, per release — and refuse the numbers this industry routinely invents.

The compounding part: the artist identity is built once and reused forever, so the second release is materially cheaper than the first — and every test result carries forward into what we generate next.
Testing through our networks

Disclosed accounts. Not simulated trends.

Every platform's rule reduces to one test: does the account deceive people about who is behind it? Not how many accounts you run. So we run the version that's openly the label's — and we retire what doesn't earn pickup.

Account tiers under 5K followers now average ~350 views per post, down 59% year over year, and a usable audience signal realistically takes 3–6 months per account. The industry's cautionary tale — an agency that admitted on record to "posting enough volume across enough accounts to simulate the idea that the song is trending" — didn't get banned. Analytics

We report what's real and refuse the rest.

There is no conversion signal flowing from Spotify back to any ad platform — tracking ends at the click. Every vendor reporting streams-per-dollar is modelling, and most won't tell you. We'd rather hand you three honest numbers than twelve invented ones.

Same with creator demographics. No platform exposes an arbitrary creator's follower breakdown — third-party tools infer it from a visible sample, and not one vendor in that market publishes an accuracy figure. Sound-usage data is measured behaviour and roughly 10× cheaper.

Plus a full cost ledger: provider, model, units, cost — per kit and per delivered asset, exported warehouse-ready. Nobody else in this category shows you the bill.

The mechanic

The second video for an artist is cheap.

Building an artist's on-screen identity — structural facial features, reference frames across lighting setups, wardrobe variants, anti-drift negatives, signed likeness release — is the expensive part, and it happens once, not once per release.

So your cost per release doesn't sit flat. It falls with every song that artist puts out. A roster releasing continuously is exactly the shape this rewards — and exactly the shape a per-video agency punishes.

Set the TikTok clip window to the hook.

At distribution you specify the 60-second window creators pick from. Leave it blank and the default is the first 60 seconds. It's a coarse lever — you set the menu, not the meal — but because "tap to use" tends to grab from the window's start, setting it at the hook biases the median creator. Confirmed across DistroKid, TuneCore, CD Baby and SoundOn; support and editability differ, so we check yours.

Fix Sound ID fragmentation before release.

Industry analysis found 2.8 million Sound IDs across 141,000 songs. A track that breaks out on the wrong Sound ID earns nothing. Metadata hygiene is unglamorous, costs nothing, and is the difference between a windfall and a windfall someone else banks.

Virality guarantees

Identical songs in identical experimental worlds produced wildly different winners. Anyone selling you a breakout is selling the ticket, not the odds.

Paid seeding

No rigorous evidence it causes durable organic pickup, credible testimony against it, and a live reputational case study. We don't buy plays, followers, engagement or accounts.

Stream-count attribution

Technically impossible — the signal doesn't exist. Any dashboard showing it is modelling and calling it measurement.
